Pharmacy Continuity Starts with Knowing Where the Database Lives

A cloud-region incident is a useful prompt for pharmacies to map which parts of billing, inventory, and patient history are local and which rely on a provider.

By Raktim Ranjit · Updated 27 October 2026 · 4 min read

The incident, and the operational question

A regional cloud service disruption can affect applications through APIs and supporting services even if the pharmacy’s own shop network is healthy. The right question is not “is cloud bad?” It is “what is our fallback for the exact workflow patients need today, and how do we reconcile it later?”

What the primary record says

Google’s preliminary incident report says a power interruption at the europe-west2-b data-centre facility began at 23:00 PDT on 19 October and the incident ended at 05:10 PDT on 20 October 2026. Its account describes a failed transfer from UPS battery power to generators after both the primary and backup programmable controllers failed to initiate the expected sequence. Physical hosts, network equipment and storage in that zone lost power; some regional products experienced elevated errors, timeouts or delayed processing. This was a provider infrastructure incident, not a report that pharmacy records were exposed. The source labels its root-cause analysis preliminary, and product impacts were not identical.

What this means for a working business

A pharmacy should map workflows, not just databases. Billing, stock lookup, batch and expiry checks, credit ledgers, patient history, appointment scheduling and external claims can sit on different systems. When a disruption occurs, staff need to know what information is essential to dispense safely, what may be recorded temporarily, and who reconciles it later. Local control can keep a database close to the counter, but it does not by itself establish the pharmacy’s legal, clinical or privacy obligations. The system’s hosting model and the pharmacy’s operating procedure must be evaluated together.

Where the product fits in the actual workflow

KinetiRx is documented as a self-hosted pharmacy and small-clinic system using PostgreSQL on operator-controlled hardware. The public project describes POS billing, medicine inventory, batch and expiry tracking, due-khata, patient records, OPD scheduling, expenses and role-based staff accounts. That architecture gives an operator a choice over where core records live and how they are backed up. It also means the pharmacy must secure the host, restrict staff access, maintain power and network equipment, patch the system and test database recovery. Optional AI integrations are separate dependencies; the core app is documented to run without a Gemini API key.

Walk through a realistic failure

For a pharmacy, start with a low-risk rehearsal: disconnect the WAN during a quiet period while keeping the local server available. Check whether staff can locate inventory and expiry information, complete a test bill and view the permitted record fields. Do not simulate dispensing without the pharmacist’s approved procedure. Then test a local server failure in a separate exercise. If a critical record is unavailable, staff need an approved clinical and regulatory path—not an improvised guess. At recovery, reconcile recorded sales, claims and stock movements against the authoritative system, with a second staff member checking the exceptions.

A practical continuity drill

Run this during a quiet period with the person who will make the decision during a real incident. Write down what you observe, what is unavailable, and how staff return to normal operation. A drill that ends with a clear owner and a measured recovery time is more useful than a checklist nobody has tried.

  • List the records staff need for routine dispensing and mark which system stores each one.
  • Document a downtime process that avoids guessing about allergies, prescriptions, stock or payment status.
  • If paper records are used temporarily, number them and define who enters and verifies them after recovery.
  • Restore a backup into a separate test environment periodically; record recovery time and the age of restored data.

Questions to answer before relying on the setup

  • Which tasks can continue safely with a local database, and which require an outside service?
  • How are patient and business records protected from unauthorized staff access?
  • Can the pharmacy restore data without relying on the same machine or credentials as production?
  • Who owns the decision to switch to and exit downtime procedures?

Scope and limitations

Local hosting does not remove risks from power loss, device failure, theft, misconfiguration, or compromised accounts. A continuity plan still needs offline backups and a tested restore.

Product documentation to verify before deployment

KinetiRx is documented as a self-hosted pharmacy and small-clinic system using a PostgreSQL database on operator-controlled hardware. The project describes billing, batch and expiry tracking, due-khata, patient records, and drawer reconciliation. That gives an operator a choice about where core records run; it also makes backup testing, access controls, and local recovery the operator’s responsibility.
